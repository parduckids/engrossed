Reading with a wandering mind

By A. Writer14 min readShareSave238 comments

Most advice about reading assumes the problem is time. Find an hour, put the phone in another room, and the pages will turn themselves. For a lot of people that is not the problem at all. The hour is there. The book is open. And somewhere around the third paragraph, the words keep moving while the mind quietly leaves.

You notice it a page later, when nothing you just read has stuck. So you go back to the top and start again, and the second attempt is harder than the first, because now it feels like a test you are failing. That loop is exhausting, and it is the real reason so many good books are abandoned a third of the way in.

Why a full page works against you

A dense page offers the eye hundreds of places to land. Every line is a fresh decision about where to look next, and every decision is a chance to drift. Readers who find this easy tend to make those decisions without noticing. Readers who don't are spending effort on every single one of them.

The page also hides your progress. Ten minutes of careful reading can move you half a page, and the rest of the chapter sits right there, unchanged, as if nothing had happened. It is very hard to stay with something that never seems to get smaller.

Smaller pieces, whole thoughts

The fix is not to read faster. It is to look at less at once. Two or three sentences fit in working memory comfortably, and they can be taken in with one look instead of a dozen. When that little piece is finished, the next one takes its place, and the only decision left is whether to carry on.

The pieces have to be whole thoughts, though. Cutting text into equal lengths would break sentences in half, and half a sentence held in your head is exactly the strain you were trying to remove. Each piece should end where a sentence ends, every time.

Keeping the thread

Moving on a few sentences at a time has a cost: the sentence before is gone. A faint echo of its last few words, sitting just above the new ones, is usually enough to keep the thread without pulling your eye away from where it needs to be.

Checking that it landed

Every few minutes, it helps to stop and say, in one line, what the last part was about. Not written down, just said to yourself. If something comes easily, carry on. If nothing comes, that is useful too. It means the last few minutes didn't land, and rereading them now costs far less than discovering it twenty pages later.

Short, bounded sessions help in the same way. Twenty minutes has an end you can see from the start. When it arrives, stand up, look at something far away, and get some water. The next session goes better if this one actually ends.

Coming back tomorrow

The last piece is the easiest to overlook. When you close a book, your place should be kept exactly, down to the sentence, and when you return it should step back a little so you land in context rather than in the middle of a thought. Starting is the hardest part of reading. It should never also mean finding where you were.
